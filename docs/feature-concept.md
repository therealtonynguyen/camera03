# Feature Concept

**Status:** Early development · Story by Zachary

> A guy finally works up the nerve to tell his best friend he loves her. But he lives in a world where blockbusters keep happening, and every one of them happens in his way.

**Working titles:** *Seen* · *Collateral* · *Detour* · *Background Character*

---

## Premise

OFF CAMERA shows famous movie moments through cameras that were never meant to matter. The feature goes one step further and asks what it is like to *live* in the background of those movies.

The main character is an ordinary guy with an ordinary goal. Every time he gets close to it, a famous scene from another film happens just out of frame. None of it is aimed at him. He is collateral damage: stuck in the detour, caught in the blackout, sitting in the restaurant that gets shut down.

He is never the hero of those stories. The film is about what it costs to be an extra in everyone else's movie, and about finally becoming the lead in your own.

---

## The Message

> You may not be the superstar or the superhero, but you are the main character of your own story. And nothing in it is wasted.

The shorts and the feature are one argument, made in stages:

| | Message | What the viewer learns |
|---|---|---|
| **OFF CAMERA shorts** | There's always another point of view | Not everyone in the frame is the main character |
| **The feature** | The guy in the background has a story too | He was the main character of his own story all along |
| **The credits** | Everyone in the background has one | Nobody in the frame is just an extra |

---

## Theme: Being Seen

The protagonist spends his life watching other people and feels invisible. The ending shows he was seen the whole time: by her, by the doorbell camera, and by something larger the film never names.

### Faith undertones

The film carries a quiet Christian undertone that is never stated. People who know will recognize it, and everyone else will feel it.

| Motif | Touchstone | How it appears |
|---|---|---|
| **The God who sees** | Genesis 16:13 | A film about cameras and watching whose central comfort is that you are seen |
| **Night-shift watchmen** | Luke 2:8 | The first people told were shepherds "keeping watch over their flock by night." The protagonist is a night-shift watchman. Never pointed out. |
| **Detours as providence** | Genesis 50:20 · Romans 8:28 | The credits reveal that every detour protected or positioned him (see *Ending*) |
| **The door** | Revelation 3:20 | The whole film builds to him standing at a door |
| **The stranger** | Hebrews 13:2 | A recurring figure at every disaster. Quietly angelic, never explained. |
| **Light** | — | At key moments a warm light finds him, through a window or a gap in the clouds. Never commented on. |

### "Nothing is wasted," not "it was all fate"

If fate did everything, his choice wouldn't matter. The detours bring him to the door, but **he still has to ring the bell.** Providence alongside free will is truer to the theology and better drama, because the climax is still his decision.

### Guardrails

- No sermons
- No scripture on screen
- No conversion scene
- No character who exists only to explain the meaning
- If a motif needs explaining, it is too loud

## Visual Style

**One house style for everything.** Every crossover gets redesigned into the film's own look. We don't bring in The Incredibles' retro design, Ratatouille's cartoon look, Finding Nemo's realism or Enchanted's live action. Mixing them would look like a collage, not a movie.

The goal is a look that belongs to this film alone, the way *Spider-Man: Into the Spider-Verse* and *KPop Demon Hunters* each built one, and that no Disney or Pixar film has used.

### Core: background to foreground

In animation, background characters are drawn simpler: fewer details, muted colors, fewer frames. The film turns that into his character arc.

| Stage | How he is drawn |
|---|---|
| **Act 1** | Drawn like an extra: muted palette, simplified linework, choppy 8–12 fps. The big events around him are in full color, rich detail and smooth motion. |
| **Act 2** | He gains color, detail and smoother motion as he steps into his own story, gradually and scene by scene |
| **Climax** | At the door, he is fully drawn for the first time. The main-character treatment. |
| **Credits** | The background extras from the whole film each get a brief moment in full color |

How detailed a character is drawn shows *how much the story sees them*. The audience gets the message without a word of dialogue.

### Camera footage: shaped by the lens

Normal scenes are painterly and warm. Surveillance shots use *stylized* compression: drawn scanlines, blocky pixel shapes and halftone noise, not fake-realistic video. This keeps the OFF CAMERA camera language inside the house style.

### Light: a touch of stained glass

In key moments (the climax, and the moments when "the light finds him"), the style shifts toward stained glass: thick dark outlines like lead lines, and color fields that glow when light passes through them. It is used sparingly, as visual language for grace.

### Production implications

- **Shorts move into the house style.** Later OFF CAMERA productions should adopt it. Each one then works as a style test and a preview of the feature, and builds the brand before fundraising.
- **AI consistency is the hard part.** Video models drift back to their default looks. A custom style needs a style guide, approved reference frames and probably a custom-trained style model. Proving consistency is part of proving the project can be made.

## Characters

### The protagonist

A **night-shift security guard who watches CCTV monitors**. For years he has seen impossible things on those screens: toys that move, a rat running a kitchen, a man lifting a train. Nobody believes him, and he has stopped telling people.

His job ties the feature straight back to OFF CAMERA. He *is* the person watching the footage.

He is careful, observant and always on the outside of things. He knows exactly what he wants to say to her. He just never quite gets there.

### The best friend

Close, funny, a little guarded. She is the one person who half-believes his stories, and she knows more than she lets on (see *Ending*). Her own life has been full of similar near-misses.

### The stranger

A figure who turns up at every disaster: in the crowd at the detour, at the next table in the restaurant, on the boardwalk. Calm while everyone else panics. Never named and never explained.

### Supporting cast (to develop)

- A coworker on the monitor shift who thinks he is making it all up
- Her family, especially a niece or nephew he is trying to win over

## Genre Blend

| Thread | Role in the story |
|---|---|
| **Romcom** | The spine: confession, interruption, misunderstanding, the last-second dash |
| **Mystery** | Why does chaos follow him? Is he cursed? Is something steering events? This holds the episodic obstacles together. |
| **Action** | He gets close enough to the big events to have to survive them, without ever being the hero |
| **Drama** | The cost of always being in the background, and choosing to step forward |

## Obstacle Map

Each obstacle is a famous movie event seen from the edge of the frame, redesigned in the house style. The third column is an **original stand-in** in case the project goes the original-IP route (see *Rights*).

### Act 1: The plan

| His plan | What gets in the way | Source | Original stand-in |
|---|---|---|---|
| Drives over to confess | Police detour around a bomb attack and a super-strong hero | The Incredibles | A retro supervillain vs. a family of supers |
| Dentist appointment to look good for the date | A pelican crashes through the office window mid-cleaning | Finding Nemo | A bird on a mission bursts into the office |
| Buys her nephew the must-have space-ranger toy | Sold out everywhere. The one he finally finds walks off on its own | Toy Story 2 | Toys that come alive when nobody is looking |

### Act 2: Getting closer

| His plan | What gets in the way | Source | Original stand-in |
|---|---|---|---|
| First real date at a celebrated Paris restaurant | Shut down by a health inspector after a rat sighting | Ratatouille | A restaurant with a secret, non-human chef |
| Romantic boardwalk night | A giant red panda wrecks a boy-band concert and the crowd stampedes | Turning Red | A teenager who turns into a giant animal |
| Quiet dinner in Times Square | A woman in a wedding gown starts a citywide musical number | Enchanted | A fairy-tale princess loose in Manhattan |
| Vacation in Hawaii to reset things | A blue "dog" and two aliens tear up the beach | Lilo & Stitch | An escaped alien experiment |
| Babysitting with her to prove he's ready for kids | The niece swears there's a monster in the closet, and she's right | Monsters, Inc. | Closet doors that open into another world |

### Act 3: All or nothing

| His plan | What gets in the way | Source | Original stand-in |
|---|---|---|---|
| The big confession, under the lights at a balloon festival | A house on balloons snags the power lines and blacks out the city | Up | A grieving widower's floating house |
| Final race to the airport | City shut down by a robot or microbot rampage | Big Hero 6 | A swarm of rogue tech |

**Setting note:** the source films need a modern, real-world-compatible setting. Worlds like *Cars*, *Zootopia* or *Frozen* don't fit one shared reality, so they are out unless the story finds a framing device.

## Structure Sketch

**Opening.** A CCTV clip of an impossible event, in OFF CAMERA style. Pull back to show him watching it on a monitor, eating a sandwich, unimpressed.

**Act 1 — The Detour**
Set up his job, his monitors and his long friendship with her. He decides tonight is the night and drives to her house. He stands on her porch, loses his nerve and leaves. The bomb detour starts a run of near-misses. Inciting realization: this isn't bad luck. It keeps happening.

**Act 2 — The Pattern**
They keep making plans and the world keeps breaking them. He goes back to her porch several times and never rings. The romcom escalates alongside the mystery: he starts tracking the incidents on his monitors and notices the stranger at every one.

*Midpoint twist:* her doorbell camera triggers on motion. It has recorded every one of his visits. She has watched clip after clip of him standing at her door and walking away. She knows, and she has been waiting for him to ring.

A misunderstanding pushes them apart. Low point: he decides he is meant to stay in the background.

**Act 3 — The Lead**
The biggest event of the film hits the city. For once, instead of waiting it out, he goes straight through it, toward her door.

## Ending

### Climax: the doorbell camera

He reaches her porch, the one he has walked away from so many times. We follow the approach in normal film coverage. As the door opens, we cut to her doorbell camera's fisheye view, with the same framing as *CAM 03 / 001: Sid's Porch*, and **hold**. No cutting back.

**We don't hear what he says.** The audio is tinny and muffled, or dropped altogether. After a whole film of interrupted attempts, the audience reads the confession from body language alone.

For the first time, he is fully drawn. The camera that watched him walk away every time catches the moment he stays. The boring camera has finally caught an extraordinary event: two ordinary people.

**Final shot:** a notification on her phone, "Motion detected at Front Door," or the motion trigger times out and the screen goes black.

**Timestamp:** the on-screen time should mean something, like a deadline or the date they met.

### Credits: the mirror reveal

Replay footage of the film's big events, each in OFF CAMERA style, and point out what nobody noticed the first time:

- **He is in the background of every shot.**
- **So is she.** She was stuck on the other side of the bomb detour. She was the other diner thrown out of the closed restaurant. They were in the same frames the whole movie and never saw each other.
- **The detours had a purpose.** The bomb detour kept him off the street that got hit. The closed restaurant sent them to the food truck where they had their best night. Nothing was wasted.
- **The stranger is in every frame too**, and is the only one looking at the camera.

The credits close with the background extras, each given a brief moment in full color.

### Alternative: the news helicopter

If the doorbell climax doesn't hold up in development: the confession happens during the film's biggest event. We cut to live news footage, with an anchor narrating the chaos and a long lens scanning the city. In the corner of the frame are two tiny figures on a street corner that the broadcast never notices.

### Risks

| Risk | Fix |
|---|---|
| **Too cold:** surveillance footage at the emotional peak feels distant | The midpoint twist about her doorbell clips. By the climax, the audience knows what that camera has witnessed. |
| **Creepy:** an archive of someone lingering at your door reads as stalking | Her side has to be warm. She was hoping he would come back, not documenting him. |
| **Gimmicky:** a camera ending tacked onto a normal film | Camera footage runs through the whole film: the opening, his monitors, the midpoint, the credits |

## Roadmap: Shorts to Feature

The OFF CAMERA shorts work as development, marketing and proof of concept at once.

| Phase | Output | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Shorts** | OFF CAMERA productions (CAM 03 / 001, 002, …) | Build skills, audience and a portfolio. Establish "there's always another point of view." |
| **2. House style** | Later shorts move into the feature's visual style | Develop and prove the look, and keep it consistent across AI tools |
| **3. The background guy** | Hide the protagonist in each short: his car in the dashcam clip, him walking past the doorbell camera | Seed the character. Fans who notice ask "who is this guy?" |
| **4. Proof-of-concept** | A short scene from the feature itself, such as the bomb detour | Show the tone, the premise, the style and that the production can deliver |
| **5. Pitch package** | Treatment, deck, style bible, proof-of-concept, the shorts and audience metrics | Fundraising, investors, festivals, studios |
| **6. Feature** | Full-length film | — |

## Rights

**Settle this before raising any money.**

The fan shorts sit in a gray area that rights holders often tolerate. A **crowdfunded or commercial feature** built on Disney/Pixar characters is different. That is commercial use of their IP, Disney is known for enforcing it, and the legal risk lands on whoever raises the money.

**Restyling doesn't solve this.** Mr. Incredible drawn in our house style is still Mr. Incredible: copyright protects the character, not the art style. Since every crossover gets redesigned anyway, though, original stand-ins become a natural fit rather than a compromise.

| Path | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|
| **Original stand-ins** *(recommended)* | Keeps the premise and the references land; Zachary owns everything; fundable and distributable | Loses literal recognition, so the stand-ins must be well designed |
| **Public domain** | Real, recognizable characters | Small pool (early Mickey, some original source texts); Disney's later versions stay protected |
| **Licensing / studio pitch** | The full vision with real characters | Long odds; requires an existing track record (the shorts help) |

Comedies like *Free Guy* and *Galaxy Quest* show that original stand-ins can carry a genre-reference premise.

Get an entertainment lawyer's read before launching any fundraising campaign.
